Teeth whitening: the honest version

Bright Smile Dental Clinic 13 September 2026 6 min read

Draft — not yet reviewed by the clinic. This article was written as a starting point and has not been checked by a dentist. Please review the wording before publishing it to patients.

Whitening is the treatment people ask us about most, and also the one surrounded by the most wishful thinking. It genuinely works. It just does not work on everything, it is not permanent, and there are situations where it is the wrong answer entirely.

Here is what is worth understanding before you spend anything.

Not all discolouration is the same

This is the distinction that decides whether whitening will help you.

Surface staining

Picked up from the outside over time — coffee, tea, red wine, cola, tobacco, and strongly coloured foods and spices. It sits on the enamel. This kind generally responds well, and some of it lifts with a professional clean before any whitening is needed at all.

Staining from within the tooth

Comes from the structure of the tooth itself rather than anything deposited on it. Causes include natural ageing — enamel thins and the yellower dentine beneath shows through — trauma to a tooth, certain antibiotics taken while teeth were forming, and excess fluoride during development.

These respond less predictably. Some improve, some barely shift, and some are better addressed another way. An examination is the only way to tell which category you are in.

The one most people do not expect

Whitening does not change crowns, veneers, fillings, bridges or implants. It only lightens natural tooth tissue. Artificial materials stay exactly the colour they were made — so if you have a white filling in a front tooth, whitening the teeth around it can leave it standing out more than before. Where that matters, the usual approach is to whiten first, let the shade settle, and then replace the restoration to match.

Health comes before colour

Whitening is not a treatment for a problem — it is a cosmetic finish applied to a mouth that is already healthy. Before it is appropriate, any active decay, gum inflammation, leaking fillings or cracked teeth need dealing with.

There is a practical reason as well as a safety one: whitening agents reaching exposed dentine or an untreated cavity are considerably more uncomfortable.

Sensitivity: what it is actually like

Some sensitivity during or shortly after whitening is common and expected. It typically feels like brief, sharp twinges — sometimes described as zingers — and a heightened reaction to cold.

For most people it settles within a day or two of finishing. It can be managed by spacing treatments out, using a desensitising toothpaste beforehand, and avoiding very hot and very cold things for a short period afterwards. Tell your dentist if you already have sensitive teeth, because the plan can be adjusted before you start rather than after.

How long it lasts

Not for ever. How long depends almost entirely on what happens afterwards — a heavy coffee or tea drinker who smokes will see change far sooner than someone who does not.

It is realistic to think of whitening as something that is topped up periodically rather than done once. Things that help it last:

  • Be careful with staining foods and drinks in the first days after treatment, when teeth take up colour most readily.
  • Drinking through a straw reduces contact with the front teeth.
  • Rinse with water after coffee, tea or wine.
  • Keep up regular cleaning and check-ups.
  • Stopping smoking makes a larger difference than any other single factor.

Shop-bought kits and charcoal

Over-the-counter products vary enormously. Some do very little; the concern with others is that a tray which does not fit your mouth lets the gel sit on the gums rather than the teeth.

Abrasive fixes — charcoal powders, whitening pastes marketed on how strongly they scrub, home remedies involving acidic fruit or bicarbonate — carry a particular risk. They can remove some surface stain, but they can also wear enamel away, and enamel does not regenerate. A tooth that has lost enamel looks yellower, because more dentine shows through. It is possible to end up further from where you started.

When whitening is not the answer

  • Discolouration caused by a single darkened tooth after trauma — usually a different treatment.
  • Front teeth largely made up of crowns or veneers — the colour is set by the material.
  • Untreated decay or gum disease — these come first.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding — normally postponed as a precaution.
  • Children and young teenagers — generally not appropriate while teeth are still developing.

Be wary of anyone promising a precise result before looking in your mouth. How much a tooth lightens depends on why it is dark in the first place, and that cannot be known from a photograph.

This article is general information about whitening, not personal dental advice, and it does not describe any specific treatment plan. Suitability, method and likely results depend entirely on an examination. If you are considering whitening, please book a consultation so your teeth can be assessed properly.
