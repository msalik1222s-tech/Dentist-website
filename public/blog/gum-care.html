Looking after your gums between visits

Bright Smile Dental Clinic 13 September 2026 5 min read

Draft — not yet reviewed by the clinic. This article was written as a starting point and has not been checked by a dentist. Please review the wording before publishing it to patients.

Most people judge their dental health by whether anything hurts. Gums do not work that way. Gum disease is usually painless in its early stages, and by the time it becomes uncomfortable it has often been developing quietly for years. That is the single most useful thing to know about them.

The good news is that the early stage is reversible, and the things that reverse it are ordinary daily habits rather than anything dramatic.

What healthy gums actually look like

Healthy gums are firm, fit snugly around each tooth, and are usually a pale pink — though natural gum colour varies between people, and some perfectly healthy gums have darker pigmentation. The more reliable signals are these:

  • They do not bleed when you brush or clean between your teeth.
  • They are not swollen, shiny or puffy along the edges.
  • They have not receded, leaving teeth looking longer than they used to.
  • Your breath is not persistently bad despite good cleaning.

Bleeding when you brush is the symptom people most often dismiss. It is common, but common is not the same as normal — healthy gums do not bleed.

The two stages worth knowing

Gingivitis

Plaque builds up along the gum line and irritates the tissue. Gums become inflamed, red and prone to bleeding. Nothing has been permanently lost at this point — with thorough cleaning and a professional scale, gingivitis can resolve completely.

Periodontitis

If inflammation continues, it can progress to affect the bone and fibres that hold teeth in place. Gums may recede, pockets form between gum and tooth, and in advanced cases teeth loosen. This stage can be managed and stabilised, but bone that has already been lost does not simply grow back — which is why catching things early matters so much.

What actually helps, day to day

None of this is complicated. The difficulty is consistency, not technique.

  • Brush twice a day, for two full minutes. Two minutes is considerably longer than most people think. Time it once and see.
  • Use a soft brush and angle it at the gum line. Scrubbing harder with a stiff brush damages gums rather than cleaning them better. Gentle, small circular movements where the tooth meets the gum.
  • Clean between your teeth once a day. A brush cannot reach the surfaces between teeth, and that is precisely where gum problems tend to begin. Floss or interdental brushes both work — the best one is the one you will actually use.
  • Do not rinse with water straight after brushing. Spit out the excess and leave it. Rinsing washes away the concentrated fluoride you just applied.
  • Replace your brush or brush head regularly. Once the bristles splay outwards, it is no longer cleaning properly.

If your gums bleed when you start cleaning between your teeth

This is common in the first week or so, because inflamed gums bleed easily. It usually settles as the inflammation reduces. If bleeding is still happening after two weeks of consistent cleaning, that is worth having looked at rather than pushing through.

Things that raise the risk

Some factors make gum disease more likely or harder to control, and they are worth mentioning to your dentist because they change how closely you should be monitored:

  • Smoking and tobacco use — one of the strongest risk factors, and it also masks bleeding, which hides the early warning sign.
  • Diabetes — the relationship runs both ways: poorly controlled blood sugar worsens gum health, and gum inflammation makes blood sugar harder to control.
  • Pregnancy and hormonal changes — gums can become more reactive and inflamed.
  • Certain medications — some cause dry mouth or gum overgrowth. Bring your list along.
  • A family history of gum disease or early tooth loss.

When to book rather than wait

Routine check-ups exist to catch things before they announce themselves. But do not wait for the next scheduled visit if you notice:

  • Gums that bleed regularly, or bleed without being touched
  • Gums that are swollen, tender or pulling away from your teeth
  • A tooth that feels loose or has changed position
  • Persistent bad taste or bad breath that cleaning does not fix
  • Any lump, ulcer or patch in the mouth that has not healed within two weeks

That last one is not about gum disease specifically, but it is the sort of thing that should always be checked promptly rather than watched.

This article is general information about dental health, not personal dental advice. Gums vary, and so do the reasons behind a symptom — only an examination can tell you what is happening in your particular case.
